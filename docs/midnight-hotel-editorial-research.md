# Midnight Hotel editorial research

Prepared October 30, 2026 for the first ten original stories, aimed approximately at ages 8–12 / Grades 4–5. The reader preferred the funny-spooky hotel sample over the observatory sample and requested stories without exercises. That direct preference is the most relevant evidence for this project.

## What the research supports

These sources inform editorial judgment; they do not establish a formula that guarantees enjoyment. Survey age ranges, countries, and collection years differ. Author interviews describe professional practice rather than controlled experiments. We have not tested the ten new stories with a representative group of children.

### Humor and unfamiliar worlds

[Scholastic, *Kids & Family Reading Report*, seventh edition, “Finding Their Story”](https://www.scholastic.com/content/dam/KFRR/Downloads/KFRReport_Finding%20Their%20Story.pdf), published 2019; US children ages 6–17. In the report, 52% selected books that make them laugh and 40% selected books that explore unfamiliar places or worlds. These are historical preferences across a broader age range, not a current popularity chart for ten-year-olds. Our application: recurring comic personalities, escalating magical mishaps, and a hotel worth exploring. Humor comes from choices and consequences rather than a disconnected joke in every paragraph.

### Interest, visual appeal, and choice

[National Literacy Trust, *Children and young people's reading in 2025*](https://literacytrust.org.uk/research-services/research-reports/children-and-young-peoples-reading-in-2025/), added June 3, 2025; early-2025 UK survey, 114,970 responses ages 5–18 overall. The motivation findings for ages 8–18 include links to favorite film/TV content (38.1%), personal interests (37.1%), interesting covers/titles (30.9%), and freedom to choose (26.6%). Our application is an original monster world matching this reader's interest, strong individual story titles and images, freely available stories, and comfortable stopping points. We are not borrowing film characters or claiming that visual polish alone causes reading enjoyment.

### Visual storytelling is legitimate reading

[National Literacy Trust, *Children and young people's engagement with comics in 2023*](https://literacytrust.org.uk/research-services/research-reports/children-and-young-peoples-engagement-with-comics-in-2023/), published March 18, 2024; early-2023 UK data from 64,066 respondents ages 8–18. The report says 49.7% of ages 8–11 read comics monthly. Respondents describe accessibility, enjoyment, and engaging adventures. Associations between comics and engagement do not establish causation. Our application: expressive scene-specific art, visual jokes, clear silhouettes, readable prose spacing, and consistent characters. Ten copies of one hotel exterior would not serve that purpose.

### A familiar cast, manageable suspense, and different formats

[BookTrust, *Bookbuzz Primary: Choosing books to get KS2 children reading for pleasure*](https://www.booktrust.org.uk/resources/find-resources/bookbuzz-primary-choosing-books-to-get-ks2-children-reading-for-pleasure/), undated guidance consulted October 30, 2026; ages 7–11. BookTrust describes varied interests and abilities, manageable scares, short chapters, humorous illustrated books, and the appeal of returning to series characters. This is expert selection guidance. Our application: familiar co-leads with distinct voices; ten different local adventures within one planned Room 13 mystery; atmospheric tension without graphic harm; four short reading sections per complete story. All ten are available without tests or locks.

### Pace, illustration, and writing that works aloud

[Cressida Cowell, *How to Train Your Reader*, BookTrust](https://www.booktrust.org.uk/resources/find-resources/how-to-train-your-reader-2/), undated first-person article consulted October 30, 2026. Cowell describes fast and unexpected plots, attention to the visual experience for older readers, and language designed for reading aloud. Our application: an immediate peculiar event, dialogue with different rhythms, moments of surprise, and thoughtful illustrations. We do not simplify every idea or define every interesting word inside the narrative. We also avoid treating her personal approach as experimental proof or imitating her prose or fictional world.

### Release tension with character-based comedy

[Scholastic interview with R. L. Stine](https://www.scholastic.com/parents/books-and-reading/raise-a-reader-blog/goosebumps-author-r-l-stine-on-inspiration-horror-and-humor.html), November 22, 2024; page marked ages 7–12. Stine discusses humor during intense scenes, outlines, and keeping notes on characters. Our application: a consistent series bible before drafting; physical and magical rules established before solutions; humor that belongs to Bark Bolt, Nia, or a guest's situation. The hotel remains warm and welcoming despite uncanny incidents. We use the craft principle without copying his prose or plots.

### Mysteries require a planned explanation

[Puffin Schools, *How to plan a detective story with Robin Stevens*](https://www.puffinschools.co.uk/resources/ks2-3-video-how-to-plan-a-detective-story-with-robin-stevens-5-mins/), undated author resource consulted October 30, 2026; KS2/3. It discusses setting, an incident, suspects, clues, and resolution, including motives and objects that connect a suspect to the incident. Our application: establish the silver ink, blue thread, room plan, brass key, hinge diagram, and maintenance song before their final use. Mallow's explanation must account for the incidents he caused and distinguish those he did not. A brand-new spell cannot replace the payoff.

### Relationships and consequences carry forward

[Katherine Arden interview, *Publishers Weekly*](https://www.publishersweekly.com/pw/by-topic/authors/interviews/article/90049-q-a-with-katherine-arden.html), August 9, 2022. Arden discusses carrying emotional consequences across a series, self-contained entries, and atmosphere rather than graphic violence. Our application: Nia's mistaken accusation is repaired explicitly; Mallow's confession does not instantly erase inconvenience or restore trust; guests helped earlier contribute to the ending. Each story resolves its immediate promise, and the tenth resolves the season's central question.

## Editorial and art decisions for this collection

- The reader, Nia, and Bark Bolt contribute different useful skills. Adults remain caring and capable.
- Local plots vary: arrival mystery, luggage chase, reflection negotiation, lift performance, laundry rescue, musical mishap, moving feast, false-sign chase, confession and repairs, cooperative finale.
- The narrator is “you,” without requiring a name, gender, or appearance. Recurring character descriptions stay consistent.
- The final room-opening mechanism is planted in earlier stories. Its success needs cooperation and practical timing rather than a test answer.
- No quizzes, prompts, grades, forced ratings, countdowns, reading locks, or streak penalties accompany these ten stories.
- Each story receives its own original illustration. Warm amber light, plum and teal settings, expressive friendly monsters, and small comic details form a coherent visual language. Avoid horror realism, threatening teeth, gore, copied franchise characters, and generated text inside artwork.
- A quality review checks continuity, cause and effect, readability, spelling, story-specific art, readable mobile layout, bookmarks, and freedom to open any story. Whether the stories are genuinely enjoyable still needs the reader's response.
